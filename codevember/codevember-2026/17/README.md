# 17 / Kelp

A WebGPU flight through a giant kelp forest. The supplied **California Kelp Forest 4K — Kenneth Pedersen** film establishes the forest, and the two later photographs guide the close-up anatomy and golden underwater lighting. The references are saved under `exports/reference/`; none are used as a background or texture. All plants and fish are geometry.

```sh
npm start
```

Open <http://localhost:8017/> in a WebGPU browser. There is no build step. The page loads Three.js revision `716d590` from jsDelivr, a day newer than Day 16's. The Node checks still use Day 16's install of `4f5d6bc` through the `node_modules` symlink; the meshlet and geometry code they exercise is the same in both revisions.

The flight is a closed route of about four minutes at 0.3 m/s (`journey.js`). It opens looking up into the forest, sinks along the clear ring between the inner grove and the outer plants into the mid-forest, about 10 m above the reef, looking up at the canopy and across the grove rather than down at the floor or out at the low-detail backdrop plants, which skip the blade solver, swims through a lone plant (the swimmer parts it), rises past a column toward the canopy and the sun, and curves back to the opening view. Positions and look targets are closed centripetal Catmull-Rom splines sharing one parameter, so the gaze keeps pace with the camera. Drag to look around (nearly straight up at the surface or down at the floor) and scroll to change depth. **W A S D** or the arrow keys swim from wherever the camera is, with **Q**/**E** to sink and rise and **Shift** to swim faster; on touch screens, spread or pinch two fingers. Swimming glides to rest like a diver and stays between the floor and just below the surface. The camera is a soft sphere of 2 m radius that pushes the kelp aside (`swimmer.js`). In the blade solver it is a soft collision constraint that moves sections part of the way out in each sweep, so blades ease away with real momentum. Each upright stalk is a chain of 64 sideways offsets, simulated on the CPU only while touched or moving: a damped string under tension, pinned at the holdfast and free at the top, pulled back toward rest by its buoyant fronds and the current. From a metre outside the sphere, a spring pushes stalk nodes away from its centre, harder the deeper they are; while the swimmer moves, the push leans away from its path, so stalks slide around the sphere's sides instead of being carried ahead of it, and a hard limit near the camera keeps them off the lens. Swimming at 1.3 m/s, a stalk eases aside at under 1 m/s and sits about 1.2 m to the side as the camera passes; tension spreads the bend over several metres, and the stalk settles about three seconds later. The offsets reach the stalks, bladders, blade bases and shadow casters through a small float texture. The sphere fades out while inspecting a frond. Ticking **Fly through** again blends back onto the flight path. **Space** pauses (looking and swimming still work), **M** toggles sound, **R** returns to the start of the flight, and **H** hides the interface. The **Settings** panel is [lil-gui](https://lil-gui.georgealways.com/), as on earlier days. **Inspect a frond** follows a moving stalk from its shaded side at close range with finer meshlet detail; **Restart the flight** returns to the start. Folders hold the water and light controls (current, water density, caustics, sun shafts), color grading (exposure, white-balance temperature and tint around the fitted dive-camera balance, saturation, contrast pivoting on mid-grey, and vignette; the defaults lower the exposure to 0.65 and warm the fitted look slightly, with contrast 1.15 and a doubled vignette), leaf translucency, and rendering (live frame rate, visible meshlets, auto resolution, pixel ratio, meshlet view). The controls are listed as plain text in the bottom left (on touch screens, just dragging and pinching), hidden with the rest of the interface by **H**. Reduced-motion preferences start the scene paused. Canvas focus intentionally has no outline.

**Display → Automatic** enables HDR when the display reports `dynamic-range: high` and the browser supports extended floating-point canvas presentation. The output module is adapted from day 16 (`display-output.js`). The canvas format is chosen at load. HDR displays get an `rgba16float` canvas in extended presentation, which carries highlights above white. SDR displays keep the 8-bit canvas: in standard presentation some browsers color-manage a float canvas differently, and SDR looked more saturated than before HDR support. On the float canvas, HDR follows the display while the page is open. **SDR** and SDR displays lower the headroom to 1 without switching the presentation. After moving the window between an SDR and an HDR display, reload to change the canvas; `?hdr` forces the float canvas. **Output** shows the active mode, and **HDR peak** sets the highlight ceiling at 1–8× SDR white (default 4×). The display transform (`display-transform.js`) keeps the SDR result: the filmic curve and the display-space saturation and contrast, clipped at SDR white. On an HDR display that result is multiplied by one gain per pixel, `1 + (peak − 1) · smoothstep(log2(L / 0.3) / 5)` for scene luminance `L` before the artistic exposure, so which parts of the scene count as highlights does not change with the look. The open water and shaded kelp, below 0.3, are unchanged. Above it the gain rises over five stops to the peak, so sun shafts and the bright surface gain range as well as the sun. In the opening view, the upper shafts gain 1.65× at the median and up to about 2.7×, and the sun core reaches the full peak. Because the three channels share one gain, every HDR color has its SDR chromaticity, and peak 1 reproduces the SDR transform exactly. The sRGB-encoded frame passes through a half-float target into FXAA, so on the float canvas values above white reach the display. `await (await import('./scripts/hdr-checks.js')).checkHDR()` runs 19 GPU checks on this path; on an SDR display, load the page with `?hdr` first. They confirm that peak 1 matches the previous SDR transform for several grades, that scene luminance up to the knee is unchanged and that highlights rise monotonically to the peak. They also confirm that every HDR color is its SDR color times one gain of at least 1. On the real frame, they confirm that the canvas carries values above white (12.5% of the opening view at peak 4, up to 1.82 encoded) and that dark and mid tones match the SDR frame (all but 0.034%, at antialiased edges). The development browser reports an SDR display, so these checks verify the data path, not the luminance of a physical HDR panel.

## Scene and light transport

- 173 upright stipes and seven floating canopy fronds carry 21,988 closed blade shells. Groves beyond a 25 m radius use fewer vertices per blade and bladder. This brings the simulated forest to 21.0 million instanced source triangles. Between 18 and 44 m from the centre, 96 backdrop holdfasts carry two or three fronds each (244 fronds, 29,651 blades) to fill the mid-distance as densely as the references. They are extra instances of the four low-detail plants, so they add no geometry memory. They sway with the current field but skip the blade solver and shadow casting, and the fish do not home on them. They stay at least 5 m from the camera path and cost about 3.4 ms per frame on the M1 Air at 0.7× scale. Each curved petiole rises into a pear-shaped bladder, and its blade begins at the upper tip. Four upright growth variants and one canopy variant share geometry. Spatial groups let the meshlet renderer choose local detail independently.
- Blades curl, cup, and twist along irregular edges, with small tears. Their surface is the labyrinthine corrugation of *Macrocystis*: folds about 1.35 cm apart and a millimetre high, running mostly along the blade with herringbone swings. The folds merge and split, generated at startup as [phasor noise](https://doi.org/10.1145/3306346.3322990) on a periodic 0.8 × 0.4 m tile. The tile stores slopes rather than heights, so normals come from the chain rule and filter correctly in the mipmaps. Folds too fine for a pixel add their slope variance to the roughness instead of disappearing. Tissue gathers in the crests, which read as dark wavy lines when a blade is backlit. Part of that extra density thickens the sheet and part scatters light away neutrally, so the lines stay olive. Ambient light arrives from every direction and spreads within the tissue, so shaded blades show the folds only faintly. A metric arc length in the spare fourth component of the blade coordinates keeps the folds at the same scale on short and long blades. The margins carry thin spines 4–9 mm long and about 2.6 cm apart, leaning toward the tip, which fade out once they would span less than about two pixels. Pigment ranges from yellow to chartreuse between plants, following the close-up references, with gentle mottling across each blade.
- Gently curved stipes and tapered petioles lead into pear-shaped gas bladders with long basal necks and short blade collars. None is a perfect tube: stipes, petioles and haptera have slightly flattened, twisting cross-sections that swell and thin along their length with faint ridges, petioles flare where they leave the stipe and each curves its own way, and each bladder bows slightly and is fuller on one side. The variation hashes values each organ already has, so every level of detail and the shadow mesh agree. Bladders are thin-walled and gas-filled: light crosses about 1.3 mm of tissue in all, so they transmit about as much as a blade and glow amber when backlit. Stalks are a little translucent too. The shadow lookup for both moves past the organ's own sunward side. The inspection camera follows the same world-space current used by the instanced geometry.
- Roots stay anchored while the buoyant fronds follow the orbital swell above the lowest few metres, lagging it slightly. Each blade is an eight-section ribbon hanging from its moving gas bladder, with physical masses and forces: tissue slightly denser than seawater, the water it entrains face-on, face-on pressure drag and skin friction. The water's acceleration pushes on it through the pressure gradient and the entrained water. Its bending stiffness comes from the cupped cross-section, so long blades are floppier than short ones, and the narrow neck lets it pivot. A limp ribbon turns broadside to a cross-flow and bends across its face, so the centreline is solved as a towed cable and each section rolls toward broadside. Joint limits (120° per hinge, 150° at the neck, 90° of twist) keep the twenty-centimetre sections within what they can represent. Compute steps are at most 20 ms. Fish contact the bent sections, and the color meshlets and both shadow cascades use the same dual-quaternion skinned pose.
- Underwater, diffuse light arrives mostly from above. Each face of a blade receives ambient light from the hemisphere it faces: up to 1.6× the old uniform value facing the surface and 0.3× facing the dim water below. Seen from below, a blade's far side faces up and glows with transmitted light. Blades transmit 76% of their diffuse energy, up from 69%. An ambient occlusion term is baked per blade cross-section, bladder and stalk ring at startup. It sums the solid angle that neighbouring blades, bladders and the stalk cover, weighted toward light from above, and is stored in the spare fourth component of the pigment attribute. It dims only ambient light, since the cascaded shadows already block direct sun. Frond interiors near the stalk fall to about 0.7 and the dense floating canopy to about 0.5, with a floor of 0.45.
- Day 16's custom lighting model now uses thin-sheet diffuse transmission, with a shared energy budget for reflected and transmitted diffuse light. Pigment sets RGB absorption; blade thickness and folds set optical depth. As fucoxanthin absorbs blue-green, a blade transmits red best, green at about half of red per millimetre and blue hardly at all, and reflects with a similar spectral shape. Beer–Lambert compounds that ratio, so overlapping blades and grazing paths deepen to amber rather than rust red. The underwater light is strongly blue-green, so this keeps the kelp golden-olive instead of green. Inside the forest, the canopy and neighbouring plants block about half of the open-water ambient light, a fitted factor on top of each plant's baked occlusion. The BTDF responds to the actual light color, intensity and shadows. Tissue/water relative IOR gives restrained Fresnel reflection. The screen-space Burley gather and its extra render targets have been removed, preserving wrinkle detail. This follows the thin-surface approach described by [Khronos](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_diffuse_transmission/README.md), with fitted thickness-dependent attenuation rather than a glTF material extension.
- A real Three.js `SunLight` casts two 4096² cascades in a shared 8192×4096 atlas, covering 75 metres of view depth. Three.js fits and snaps the cascades to the camera and blends their transition. Both cascades render every frame at the current blade animation time. The near cascade covers about 49 m at the desktop viewport (about 1.2 cm per texel, versus 2.4 cm in the preceding revision and 12.6 cm originally). Lower-detail casters retain every stem, bladder, blade, instance transform and current deformation. Leaves and fish receive SunLight shadows through a deterministic four-tap rotated-grid PCF; the default filter's per-pixel noise rotation needs temporal accumulation, which this experiment does not use. The volume's compute pass samples the same atlas with four-texel bilinear PCF and the same cascade blending. No shadow frames are skipped. Hollow gas bladders use their thicker wall profile for absorption.
- Day 10's water caustics are adapted in `caustics.js`, and its direct-light modulation is reused in `caustic-lighting.js`. The surface refracts photon sheets onto eight receivers 1, 2.5, 4.5, 7.5, 11.5, 16.5, 23.5 and 33.5 metres down, stored as single-channel 512² tiles in one 2048×1024 atlas. Each sheet covers its 12 m tile plus the largest sideways refraction offset at that depth, so few photons fall outside the tile. Area ratios concentrate and spread irradiance. Shallow sheets use 224² cells to resolve the shortest waves. A wave that has folded well past its focus is incoherent at a given depth and finer than the photon grid. That sheet drops the wave and applies its sideways ray displacement as blur instead. The blur also includes the sun's 0.4° disk as seen underwater, so the sharp network a few metres down softens into broad bands by the floor. The two deepest sheets keep only waves that have not yet focused. They use a smooth per-photon Jacobian, so the wide blur does not print the photon grid. Receivers interpolate between depths along the mean solar ray. The maps update every frame and modulate direct sunlight on kelp, fish and the floor, plus the light scattered through the water. Ambient fill and emission are unaffected.
- The same sea defines the visible water surface and photon refraction: 28 waves on the 12 m lattice from 12 m swell down to 0.3 m chop, with equal slope per band (a Phillips-like spectrum) and wider directional spread for shorter waves. The floating canopy damps waves shorter than about 1.5 m, giving an RMS slope of 0.12, calmer than open water at 3 m/s wind in [Cox and Munk](https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSA.44.000838). Waves travel at 0.75× deep-water dispersion speed. The analytic slope drives dielectric Fresnel reflection, Snell refraction, and total internal reflection at the air-water boundary. Waves shorter than a pixel are filtered out, and their slope variance spreads the Fresnel, refraction and sun glint instead of aliasing. Seen from below, sixteen capillary ripples of 5–22 cm are added to the surface optics only. A pixel's footprint on the surface is longer along the view than across it, so each ripple is filtered by the footprint along its own direction. The ripples are long-crested sines, which read as ruled lines when only a few pixels wide, so one counts as resolved only from about 20 pixels per wavelength, near the surface; until then its slope variance becomes soft glitter. Unresolved slopes are averaged over four facets, each of which blurs the onset of total internal reflection over its share of the slope spread, so the edge of Snell's window does not break into flat bands. They gather on the crests of the longer waves and follow capillary–gravity dispersion, breaking Snell's window into glitter and dark streaks; their caustics would focus within a metre, so the photon maps leave them out. The sky seen through the window brightens and whitens toward the horizon, and the sun is surrounded by an aureole from forward scattering in air. The aureole is what fills the window around the sun with rippled light. The diffuse water solve keeps its simpler two-colour sky, so the underwater light level is unchanged. The sky and underwater reflection environment are analytic approximations.
- The forest contains 1,050 blue-gray blacksmiths, 240 copper-colored señoritas and 24 brilliant orange garibaldi. Besides two groups crossing the opening view, eight blacksmith and three señorita schools wait a few metres along the camera's gaze at set moments of the flight (`fish.js`), so its long middle meets fish too; like all fish they part around the camera. Garibaldi (*Hypsypops rubicundus*, a damselfish like the blacksmith) are deeper-bodied, with a shallow-forked, round-lobed tail and translucent orange fins; each is solitary and holds a station half a metre to two metres above the reef, a metre or two from a holdfast, wandering little and never rising into the water column. Both have anatomically distinct body profiles and fins. The blacksmith is deep and oval, with a deeply forked tail and a dorsal fin of fourteen notched spines followed by a taller soft part. The señorita is a slender cigar with a pointed snout, long low dorsal and anal fins and a square tail. Their cross-sections are superelliptic, with full shoulders and a narrow keel. The skin is procedural TSL in body coordinates. It has overlapping scales with pigmented margins and relief, countershading, the gill-cover seam and lateral line, and translucent membranes between opaque fin rays. The dark backs are rougher and less reflective than the silvered flanks and belly. Pattern detail fades with its screen footprint, so distant fish stay stable without temporal antialiasing. Nearby fish resolve the iris, pupil and lip. The more distant mesh keeps the silhouette and pectoral motion. Four indirect draws use the current camera frustum and a projected-length threshold of 18 render pixels. Stable ordering keeps overlapping fins consistent between frames. Blacksmith spotting and the señorita tail saddle follow the [Aquarium of the Pacific’s blacksmith description](https://www.aquariumofpacific.org/onlinelearningcenter/species/blacksmith) and [Monterey Bay Aquarium’s señorita description](https://www.montereybayaquarium.org/animals-the-ocean/animals-a-to-z/senorita).
- Blacksmiths hold loose stations in the water column facing into the current. Every few seconds most of them dart at a plankter, mostly upstream, at about seven body lengths per second for a fifth of a second, then coast. Señoritas forage within a couple of metres of the kelp. Fish steer around the swaying stipes, whose centrelines come from the forest geometry. Each fish reads its neighbors from a common start-of-step snapshot of the blade-contact grid. Separation, alignment and loose cohesion are scaled to body length; current drag and reciprocal blade forces also act. Body orientation follows swimming velocity relative to the water. Both species are labriform swimmers: they cruise by rowing the pectoral fins at 1.3 Hz plus 0.9 Hz per body length per second. Above two to four body lengths per second, or when accelerating hard, body and tail strokes take over at about 0.7 body lengths per beat. The pectorals then fold against the flank and the dorsal and anal fins lower. The body wave follows the amplitude envelope measured on swimming fish (Videler and Hess 1984, mackerel), with a wavelength of about one body length and the head yawing at a fifth of the tail amplitude. The caudal rays trail the peduncle. The gill covers and mouth pulse with ventilation. The pectoral fins row about their shoulders, twist back on recovery and open differently during a turn. The material receives the forest’s cascaded shadows and caustics. The fish, kelp and floor are lit by and reflect `scene.environment`: a 128² cube map of the water's radiance at the camera's depth (`water-environment.js`), which Three.js prefilters into a PMREM. It holds the solved diffuse field plus the skylight inside the Snell window, using the solver's surface boundary attenuated along the slant path; the water pass adds the water to the view, so a cube render of the scene itself would show unlit kelp on black. The map is rendered once at startup for the opening depth of 12.5 m. It does not follow the camera as the flight sinks to about 22 m and rises again, nor when swimming or diving, nor the water-density slider. The kelp's reverse side receives the same environment's irradiance from the opposite hemisphere through the sheet. The hemisphere fill light also reflects on physical materials since [#35580](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35580). Light transmitted through thin fin membranes uses an approximation of the surrounding water radiance.
- Marine snow drifts through the water (`marine-snow.js`): 16,000 aggregates in a 12 m box that wraps around the camera, carried by the mean current and the swell's orbital motion, sinking slowly. Each speck scatters only the direct sunlight that reaches it, with the cascaded shadows (read with texel loads, since the lighting runs per vertex) and caustics, mostly forward, so specks glint in the beams and caustic patches and vanish in the kelp's shade. That light is raised eightfold, an appearance gain rather than optics, so lit specks read at screen resolution away from the sun as well. They are drawn in their own pass after the water composite, so each is attenuated by the water between it and the camera rather than the background's distance, and a manual test against the scene depth hides them behind kelp and fish. Specks are drawn at least 1.5 pixels wide with their light spread over that area, so distant ones dim instead of flickering, and they fade out within a metre of the lens and near the box faces. They are drawn 2–8 mm across, larger than most real aggregates, which would fall far below a pixel at screen resolution. The pass costs about 0.1 ms on this M1.
- Sound is procedural Web Audio (`soundscape.js`), starting with the first click or key press as browsers require: a low, filtered surge whose loudness follows the water's orbital speed at the camera, louder near the surface, with a softer swish above it, and a sparse crackle of faint clicks above 3 kHz spread across the stereo field. **M** or **Settings → Sound** toggles it; it suspends while the tab is hidden.
- A slow, continuous orbit passes through a reserved corridor. Dragging changes the viewing direction without interrupting flight.

Water applies Beer–Lambert extinction to each camera path and to the direct sunlight that reaches an object. The coastal preset uses RGB absorption of `[0.144, 0.041, 0.027] m⁻¹`, particle scattering of `[0.053, 0.074, 0.078] m⁻¹`, and molecular scattering of `[0.0014, 0.0027, 0.0058] m⁻¹`. At the default density of 0.8, a 20 m path transmits approximately 4%, 15%, and 17% of the unscattered red, green, and blue object light. Particles have narrow and broad forward scattering lobes and a backward lobe. Their combined phase function integrates to one and sends 3.8% of particle-scattered energy into the rear hemisphere. Molecules use a Rayleigh phase function.

The [Monterey comparison scene](http://127.0.0.1:8019/) suggested sampling the volume at multiple distances along the same camera ray. Sun shafts are caustic filaments and canopy shadows stretched along the refracted solar ray, so on screen they converge on the sun's vanishing point. Their brightness changes slowly along screen lines through that point and quickly across them. A compute pass therefore marches 1,024 of these epipolar lines with 64 rays each, rather than a coarse screen grid ([Engelhardt and Dachsbacher](https://doi.org/10.1145/1730804.1730823)). Each ray stores sunlight and canopy visibility at 16 logarithmically spaced ranges up to 100 m. Steps are about 0.1 m near the camera. Each frame the pass integrates the current SunLight cascades and caustics to those ranges, with the caustic map prefiltered to the step and line spacing. A blade or fish samples the surrounding two ranges at its actual depth. The shader applies that visibility to the analytic integral for its exact path. The diffuse field comes from a water-column solve at startup. A fitted 22% of this field responds to the canopy visibility, leaving skylight in the shadows. The surface boundary uses Fresnel reflection and Snell refraction.

Light reflected by kelp, fish and the floor can scatter into adjacent view directions on its way to the camera. The sharp component uses full beam extinction at full scene resolution. A separate low-resolution pass transports the change in radiance caused by a visible object, including the diffuse light it blocks. The particle phase function is split into three forward lobes: within 5.4° (41% of particle scattering, 3° rms), 5.4–20° (32%, 12° rms) and 20–90° (23%, 45° rms). Light whose deflections all stay inside a lobe and the narrower ones survives with exp(−(c − bP)d). Differences between these nested transmissions give the energy each lobe alone redistributes, and they sum to the whole forward budget, so no light is created or lost. The narrow lobe's spread follows its angular variance and the expected number of particle encounters. The halo and glow lobes reuse the narrow pass, whose luminance-to-alpha ratio records each pixel's range. They re-weight it in 4×4 blocks and blur it at 1/8 and 1/32 of the render resolution with fixed angular widths. Before the extra lobes, roughly a fifth of an object's light at 10–20 m was dropped; it now arrives as a soft halo and glow. The receiving ray's length limits how much of that contribution reaches the camera in front of a nearby surface. Distant highlights and dark silhouettes soften as their scattered fraction grows.

The display grade is matched to a vivid close-up reference: a fixed dive-camera white balance of (2.68, 1.26, 1.08) before a filmic shoulder, then a saturation boost that favours warm hues. Blue water gets 1.06× and sunlit kelp up to 1.3×. On sampled frames the kelp then measures about 60° hue and 0.93 saturation, and the water 186° and 0.8, against 61°/0.87 and 186°/0.76 in the reference.

Antialiasing is **spatial FXAA after tone mapping and sRGB conversion**. There is no TAA/TRAA, projection jitter, motion-vector buffer, or accumulated image history. Volume samples do not change randomly between frames. Paused-frame pixel equality is checked in the browser.

The water is a real-time approximation to radiative transfer. The diffuse solve assumes a horizontally uniform column and uses azimuth-averaged sunlight as the source for repeated scattering. It omits local geometry from that diffuse solve, while the direct solar term uses the moving canopy shadows. A local modulation of the solved diffuse field approximates the loss of skylight beneath the canopy. The camera's small-angle transport uses the visible geometry and three Gaussian lobes fitted to the forward phase function; the widest assumes the scene continues beyond the frame edge. Occlusion in that term is approximated from the first visible surface on each ray. RGB coefficients are fitted to the appearance of the coastal water in the references. The supplied footage does not include optical measurements. Caustics retain their periodic 12 m field and eight discrete receiver depths. Wave folding beyond the coherence limit is represented by blur rather than traced.

The transport follows [Akkaynak and Treibitz on underwater image formation](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018/papers/Akkaynak_A_Revised_Underwater_CVPR_2018_paper.pdf) and [Jaffe on small-angle forward scattering](https://tos.org/oceanography/article/underwater-optical-imaging-status-and-prospects). The optical preset is informed by [Pope and Fry’s measurements of water absorption](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18264420/) and [Mobley et al. on marine scattering phase functions](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-41-6-1035).

## Validation and performance

```sh
npm test
```

The [volume checks](exports/validation/coastal-volume-browser.json) read back the shaft texture and confirm that all zero-length rays sample the same point, visibility changes with range and the volume updates after the cascades every frame. The [camera test](exports/validation/coastal-spectral-scattering.json) measures edge spread at 1 and 30 m. The water solver tests verify normalized phase functions, backscatter fractions, diffuse boundaries, and nonnegative in-scatter along its characteristics. The geometry checks cover finite geometry and normals, topology, preservation of every triangle and interpolant during spatial partitioning, matching organs in the lower-detail shadow geometry, the full camera path's root clearance, and fish clearance from the camera. Browser checks verify caustic/shadow/scene time synchronization and stable paused pixels. `scripts/validate-transmission.js` renders isolated HDR material samples to verify thickness attenuation, pigment filtering, light scaling, darkness without illumination, caustic suppression in shadows and the diffuse energy split. `scripts/validate-caustics.js` reads the actual GPU photon maps: flat-water irradiance remains one at every depth, the focused maps retain 97.2–99.9% of the input energy, contrast falls with depth below the focal depths, and paused maps are byte-identical. `tests/water.test.mjs` checks that the wave spectrum tiles every 12 m with unique wave vectors and a canopy-damped slope spectrum. `scripts/validate-caustic-receivers.js` additionally checks world-space atlas sampling, depth interpolation, the surface fade, and volume modulation. It compares the actual meshlet kelp and instanced fish with caustics on and off in linear HDR, identifying sunlit pixels with a separate ambient-only render. This catches receiver bugs that isolated map and material checks miss. Browser checks and measured flight reports are saved in `exports/validation/`.

`scripts/validate-water-simulation.js` reads the compute buffers to measure blade tip length, displacement bounds and fish camera clearance. An isolated blade and fish test separates current drag from the contact force and checks that the two bodies respond in opposite directions. The browser check verifies that one simulation step is shared by the shaded surface, both cascades, and the caustic render. See [the simulation report](exports/validation/gpu-water-simulation.json).

On this **Apple M1 MacBook Air (7 GPU cores, 8 GB)** in Chrome 157, the revision with **two 4096² SunLight cascades, corrected four-depth caustic sampling, diffuse transmission and spatial FXAA** measured **37.56 fps average**, **33.44 fps in the slowest one-second window**, and **33.4 ms at the 95th percentile**, at a **1280 × 720 CSS viewport** and **0.7 render scale**. A 15-second warmup precedes the complete 90-second measured orbit. There were no resolution changes during that run. See [`flight-caustic-receivers-fixed.json`](exports/validation/flight-caustic-receivers-fixed.json) for every measurement window. Short volume paths use fewer samples to recover headroom with the corrected caustic lookup. Shadow meshlets omit unused pigment and tissue streams, reducing their packed vertex bandwidth by 40% while retaining normals required by the meshlet path. Earlier reports are retained as development history; `flight-caustics-4k-shadows.json` predates the receiver fix and does not measure working caustic projection. These are measured steady-state results, not a promise that every individual frame is under 33.3 ms.

The epipolar sun shafts and eight-depth caustics cost more GPU time. Timed in isolation on this device, the caustic maps take about 4.1 ms instead of 3.0 ms, and the shaft march about 1.5 ms instead of 0.45 ms for the old 16-slice atlas. In alternating 6-second flights at fixed 0.7× scale with the same fish and forest, the median frame went from 35.2 to 39.0 ms. Another browser was using the GPU during those runs, so individual windows varied by about ±4 ms. Auto resolution absorbs the difference.

With the earlier water transport and camera-path forward scattering, the 90-second flight averaged **40.89 fps**, with **33.88 fps in the slowest one-second window** and a **33.8 ms 95th-percentile frame time**. The viewport was 1280 × 720 at a stable 0.7 render scale. See [the flight report](exports/validation/flight-water-camera-scattering.json). The renderer starts at the browser's device pixel ratio, limited by the GPU's maximum texture dimension. The forward-scattering buffers have CSS-pixel budgets, since that effect varies more slowly than the surface detail. The sun-shaft rays are fixed per epipolar line and do not depend on resolution. **Auto resolution** leaves headroom for denser groves by targeting 35 fps. It estimates how much of each frame is spent outside the pixel passes, probes a larger scale reduction, and compares the resulting frame times. The **Pixel ratio** slider fixes a requested ratio up to native DPR. Re-enabling Auto tries native again.

To repeat the complete accelerated flight benchmark from the browser console:

```js
const { startBenchmark } = await import('./scripts/benchmark.js');
const report = await startBenchmark(90, 15);
console.table({ meanFps: report.meanFps, p95Ms: report.p95Ms,
  minimumOneSecondFps: report.minimumOneSecondFps });
```

This measures the normal animation loop, including camera motion, fish updates, current deformation, shadows, spatial antialiasing, and adaptive resolution. The first 15 seconds warm the pipeline on a faster orbit, then a fresh complete orbit is measured for 90 seconds. The report includes individual one-second windows and resolution changes. Keep the tab visible and avoid video decoding or other GPU-heavy work during the measurement.

**Startup.** Meshlet clustering for the 64 kelp geometries (1.8 million source triangles, including the shadow casters) used to take 3.4 s on the main thread. It now runs in a pool of up to six module workers (`meshlet-worker.js`). Each spatial chunk is sent to a worker as soon as `createForest()` splits it, while the main thread generates the rest of the forest. `meshlet-build.js` is the CPU half of Three.js's `createMeshlets()` at the pinned revision, and `tests/meshlets.test.mjs` checks that it produces byte-identical vertex, index and cluster arrays. A dedicated worker only starts once the page's main thread yields, so `kelp-pipeline.js` waits up to 250 ms during module evaluation for the workers to boot. The blade surface tile is generated in its own worker (`tissue-worker.js`), with the same bytes as before. Preload links fetch the Three.js modules in parallel. In headless Chrome on this M1, the time from DOM ready to the first frame fell from 5.7–9.6 s to 2.3–2.6 s in three alternating runs on the same scene; other sessions were using the machine. Most of what remains is forest generation on the main thread (about 0.8 s), then shader compilation and the first frame (about 1.4 s).
